## Top Story: Trump Orders Hormuz Blockade After Peace Talks Collapse

The weekend's biggest development: US-Iran peace talks in Pakistan collapsed, and President Trump announced a full US naval blockade of all Iranian ports and coastal areas, effective 10 AM ET today (Monday).

**What happened:**
- Iran refused to yield on nuclear program demands and free passage through the Strait of Hormuz
- Trump announced the blockade on Truth Social, saying the US Navy will prevent Iran from shipping oil
- US will also "interdict" foreign ships that paid Iran's illegal $2M tolling system
- US Central Command confirmed the blockade and said minesweeping drones will clear Iranian sea mines

**Market impact:**
- Oil surged back above $100/barrel (WTI ~$105, Brent ~$103)
- Stock futures fell sharply on escalation fears
- Bond yields edged higher as inflation expectations rise

**Risk:** Iran warned it will treat military vessels approaching the strait as a ceasefire violation. The move creates more opportunity for escalation between the US Navy and Iran's small naval fleet.

**Silver lining:** US officials said negotiations were "cordial," and Iran indicated future talks remain possible.

## Concept of the Day: Naval Blockade (Economic Warfare)

A **naval blockade** is when a country uses its military to prevent ships from entering or leaving another country's ports. It's one of the strongest economic warfare tools short of direct combat.

**Why it matters for markets:**
- Iran exports ~1.5 million barrels of oil per day — blocking this tightens global supply
- The Strait of Hormuz handles ~20% of global oil traffic normally
- Even the *threat* of disruption causes oil prices to spike (as we're seeing)
- Higher oil = higher inflation = harder for the Fed to cut rates

**Historical context:** The last major US naval blockade was during the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). In trade terms, blockades are considered acts of war under international law, which is why Iran called it a ceasefire violation.

**Connection to today:** The blockade creates a feedback loop — higher oil prices hurt consumers and businesses, which could tip the economy toward recession, but inflation from oil makes it hard for the Fed's new chair Kevin Warsh to cut rates even if growth slows. This is the "vice" analysts are talking about.
