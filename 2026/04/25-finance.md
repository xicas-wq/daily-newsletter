## Concept of the Day: Dollar Swap Lines

**What are they?** A swap line is an agreement between the Federal Reserve and a foreign central bank to exchange currencies. Basically, the Fed lends dollars to another country's central bank so that country's banks can access dollar funding.

**Why do they exist?** Much of global trade and finance runs on US dollars. When there's a crisis, dollars can become scarce outside the US, causing financial panic. Swap lines are like an emergency dollar ATM for other countries.

**How they worked before:** During the 2008 financial crisis, the Fed extended swap lines to major allies (Europe, Japan, UK) and some emerging markets. At the peak, nearly $600 billion was deployed. The logic was simple: dollar shortages abroad could blow back and hurt US banks.

**What's new now:** The Trump administration is considering swap lines for **Persian Gulf states** (UAE, Qatar, Bahrain) affected by the Iran war's closure of the Strait of Hormuz. This would be unprecedented — using swap lines as a **geopolitical tool** to help wartime allies, rather than just to prevent financial contagion.

**Connection to today:** This is happening as incoming Fed Chair Kevin Warsh explicitly said "international finance" is an area where the Fed should collaborate more with the executive branch. If approved, it would mark a significant evolution in how the US uses its financial power — blurring the line between monetary policy and foreign policy.
