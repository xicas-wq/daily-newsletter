## Top Financial Stories

### 1. March Inflation Surges to 3.3% on Energy Shock
US CPI came in hot at 3.3% year-over-year for March, well above the 2.8% in February. The spike was driven almost entirely by **energy prices** — gasoline, heating oil, and electricity all surged as the US-Iran conflict disrupted Middle Eastern oil flows. Core inflation (excluding food and energy) remained more stable at around 2.9%, suggesting the underlying economy isn't overheating — it's an external supply shock.

**Why it matters:** This puts the Federal Reserve in a tough spot. Normally, high inflation means "keep rates high." But if the inflation is caused by a temporary war-related oil shock (not consumer demand), cutting rates to help a slowing economy might still be appropriate. The Fed meets again in May.

### 2. Iran Ceasefire: Fragile but Market-Moving
A ceasefire deal between the US and Iran was announced mid-week, sparking the rally. But by Friday, Iran's semi-official Tasnim news agency said **"excessive" US demands** were hindering a lasting agreement. Oil markets remain on edge — if the ceasefire collapses, expect crude to spike back toward $100/barrel.

### 3. Big Bank Earnings Week Ahead
This coming week is huge: **JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, and Citigroup** all report Q1 earnings. Investors will be watching closely for:
- How banks handled the volatility from the Iran conflict
- Loan loss provisions (are banks bracing for recession?)
- Trading revenue (volatility usually means big trading profits)

### 4. IMF/World Bank Meetings Overshadowed by War
The spring meetings of the IMF and World Bank in Washington are being dominated by the **economic fallout from the Middle East conflict**. Developing nations dependent on oil imports are facing severe budget pressures, and there's growing concern about a global slowdown.

### 5. SEC May Scrap Quarterly Reporting
The SEC is reportedly preparing to **eliminate the requirement for public companies to file quarterly earnings reports**, according to the Wall Street Journal. This would be a massive change — companies might only need to report semi-annually. Proponents say it reduces short-termism; critics worry it reduces transparency.

## Concept of the Day: Consumer Sentiment Index

The **University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index** just hit an all-time low. But what is it?

It's a monthly survey of about 500 US households that asks people how they feel about:
- Their current financial situation
- The economy's direction over the next year
- The economy's direction over the next 5 years

The results are compiled into a number (baseline = 100, set in 1966). Higher means people feel optimistic; lower means pessimistic.

**Why traders care:** Consumer spending makes up about 70% of US GDP. If people *feel* poor or worried, they spend less — which can actually cause the slowdown they're worried about. It's a self-fulfilling prophecy economists call a **"confidence shock."**

**Current reading:** The index has plunged because of the combination of war anxiety, rising gas prices, and political uncertainty. But here's the nuance: sentiment surveys don't always predict actual spending. During COVID, sentiment crashed but spending on goods boomed (thanks to stimulus checks). So take it as a signal, not a certainty.

**Connection to today's market:** The all-time low sentiment reading on Friday is why the S&P 500 slipped despite a strong week. Even with a ceasefire rally, consumers are deeply anxious — and that could eventually slow the economy if it persists.
